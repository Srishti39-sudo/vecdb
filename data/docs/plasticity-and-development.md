# Plasticity and Development

## A brain that rewires

Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience. It operates throughout life, through the strengthening and weakening of synapses, the growth and removal of dendritic spines, and changes in myelin.

A striking example comes from London taxi drivers, who must memorise thousands of streets to pass an exam known as the Knowledge. In 2000 Eleanor Maguire and colleagues reported that their posterior hippocampus, a region involved in spatial memory, was larger than in control subjects, and that its size correlated with years spent driving a taxi. A later study that followed trainees found that those who qualified showed growth over the training period.

## Critical periods

Some kinds of plasticity are strongest during windows in early life called critical periods. In the 1960s David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel showed that if one eye of a kitten was kept closed during an early window, the visual cortex rewired to favour the open eye, and vision through the deprived eye stayed poor even after it was opened. The same deprivation in an adult cat had little effect. They shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their work on the visual system.

Similar sensitive periods exist in humans. This is why childhood cataracts are treated early, and why children who learn a language young usually acquire a native accent more easily than adults.

## Pruning

The infant brain overproduces synapses. Synapse density in parts of the cortex peaks in early childhood and then falls as connections that are rarely used are removed, a process called synaptic pruning. Pruning continues through adolescence, especially in the prefrontal cortex. It follows a use-it-or-lose-it principle: active connections are kept and strengthened, while unused ones are eliminated, which makes the remaining circuits more efficient.

## New neurons in adults?

Whether adult humans grow new neurons is genuinely debated. Neurogenesis in the adult hippocampus is well established in rodents. In 2018 two human studies reached opposite conclusions: one reported that new neuron production in the human hippocampus drops to undetectable levels in adulthood, while another reported that it continues into old age. Differences in tissue handling and methods are thought to explain part of the disagreement, and the question remains open.

## Recovery after injury

After a stroke, surviving regions can take over some functions of the damaged tissue. Rehabilitation exploits this. In constraint-induced movement therapy, the healthy arm is restrained so that the patient is forced to use the weakened arm intensively, which drives reorganisation in the motor cortex and can improve function even years after the stroke.
