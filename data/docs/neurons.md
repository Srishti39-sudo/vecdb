# Neurons and Signalling

## The cells that think

The human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons, a figure measured in 2009 by dissolving brains into a uniform soup of cell nuclei and counting samples of it. Glial cells are about as numerous as neurons, not ten times more numerous as older textbooks claimed. Glia insulate axons, regulate the chemical environment around synapses, and clear debris.

A neuron has three working parts. Dendrites are branching inputs that collect signals from other cells. The cell body adds those signals up. The axon is a single long output cable that carries the result to other neurons, sometimes over a distance of a metre, as in the nerves running from the spinal cord to the foot.

## The action potential

At rest, the inside of a neuron sits at about minus 70 millivolts relative to the outside, because ion pumps keep sodium outside the cell and potassium inside. When enough input pushes the voltage past a threshold, sodium channels snap open, sodium rushes in, and the voltage briefly flips positive. Potassium channels then open and restore the resting state.

This spike is all-or-nothing: a neuron either fires a full action potential or none at all. The strength of a stimulus is therefore coded by how often a neuron fires and how many neurons fire, not by the size of individual spikes. After each spike a short refractory period stops the signal from travelling backwards.

## Myelin and speed

Many axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating sheath made by glial cells. Myelin lets the signal jump between gaps in the sheath called nodes of Ranvier, a process called saltatory conduction. Myelinated axons conduct at up to about 120 metres per second, while thin unmyelinated fibres manage around one metre per second.

When myelin is damaged, signals slow down or fail. In multiple sclerosis the immune system attacks myelin in the brain and spinal cord, which is why symptoms can include numbness, weakness and problems with vision.

## Synapses and neurotransmitters

Neurons do not usually touch. They communicate across a tiny gap called the synapse. When a spike reaches the end of an axon, it triggers the release of chemical messengers called neurotransmitters, which cross the gap and bind to receptors on the next cell.

Glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter, making the receiving neuron more likely to fire. GABA is the main inhibitory one, making it less likely to fire. The balance between excitation and inhibition keeps activity in a useful range; too little inhibition can lead to seizures. A single neuron can receive thousands of synaptic inputs.

## An expensive organ

The brain is about 2 percent of body weight but uses roughly 20 percent of the body's energy at rest. Most of that energy goes into pumping ions back across membranes after neurons signal, restoring the gradients that the next action potential will spend. Because the brain stores very little fuel, it depends on a constant supply of glucose and oxygen from the blood, and loses consciousness within seconds when blood flow stops.
