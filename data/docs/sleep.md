# Sleep and the Brain

## The architecture of a night

Sleep is not one state. It alternates between non-REM sleep, which has three stages from light to deep, and REM sleep, named after the rapid eye movements that accompany it. The deepest non-REM stage is called slow-wave sleep because large populations of neurons fire in slow, synchronised waves.

A full cycle through these stages takes about 90 minutes, and a typical night contains four to six cycles. Slow-wave sleep dominates the first half of the night, while REM periods grow longer towards the morning. During REM sleep the brain is highly active and most vivid dreaming occurs, but the muscles of the body are actively paralysed, which stops us from acting out our dreams.

## Two forces that time sleep

Two processes decide when we feel sleepy. The first is sleep pressure, which builds the longer we are awake. One contributor is adenosine, a by-product of energy use that accumulates in the brain during waking and is cleared during sleep. Caffeine keeps people alert mainly by blocking adenosine receptors.

The second is the circadian clock, a roughly 24-hour rhythm set by a small region of the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Light reaching the eyes keeps this clock aligned with the day. In the evening, as light fades, the pineal gland releases melatonin, a hormone that signals night to the rest of the body.

## Sleep and memory

Sleep strengthens memories formed during the day. During slow-wave sleep the hippocampus replays recent experiences in compressed bursts called sharp-wave ripples, and these are coordinated with sleep spindles and slow oscillations in the cortex. This coordinated replay is thought to transfer and stabilise memories in the cortex. In experiments, people who sleep after learning typically remember more than people who stay awake for the same amount of time.

## Cleaning the brain

The brain has no conventional lymphatic vessels. A 2013 study in mice found that during sleep the space between brain cells expanded by about 60 percent, allowing cerebrospinal fluid to flow through brain tissue and wash out waste products, including beta-amyloid, the protein that forms plaques in Alzheimer's disease. This proposed clearance route was named the glymphatic system. The evidence comes mainly from mice, and how strongly it operates in humans is still being studied.

## What sleep loss does

Missing sleep impairs attention first. Sleep-deprived people suffer brief lapses, often without noticing them, and their reaction times become erratic. One study found that after around 17 to 19 hours without sleep, performance on some tasks was as poor as with a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05 percent. Chronic short sleep is also linked to worse mood regulation, because the emotional centres of the brain react more strongly when they have not been rested.
