# Learning and Memory

## Cells that fire together wire together

In 1949 the psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them grows stronger. This idea, usually summarised as "cells that fire together wire together", is the core of how the brain is thought to store experience: learning is a change in the strength of synapses.

The first direct evidence came in 1973, when Tim Bliss and Terje Lømo showed that a burst of high-frequency stimulation in the rabbit hippocampus made a synapse respond more strongly for hours afterwards. This effect is called long-term potentiation, or LTP. Its mirror image, long-term depression, weakens synapses that are active out of step.

## The NMDA receptor

A key molecule in LTP is the NMDA receptor, a glutamate receptor that acts as a coincidence detector. At rest its channel is blocked by a magnesium ion. It opens only when glutamate is bound and the receiving neuron is already depolarised, that is, when the sending and receiving cells are active at the same time. Calcium then flows in and triggers changes that strengthen the synapse, including adding more receptors. In this way the receptor turns Hebb's rule into chemistry.

## Patient H.M. and the hippocampus

In 1953 a young man named Henry Molaison, known in the scientific literature as patient H.M., had parts of both medial temporal lobes removed, including most of the hippocampus, to treat severe epilepsy. The seizures improved, but he could no longer form new long-term memories of facts or events. He could hold a conversation, yet minutes later had no memory of it.

Studies by Brenda Milner showed that H.M. could still learn new motor skills. Over several days he got better at tracing a star while looking at it only in a mirror, even though each day he did not remember practising. This revealed that memory is not one system: the hippocampus is needed to form new conscious memories of facts and events, but skill learning depends on other circuits.

## Kinds of memory

Declarative memory is memory you can state. It includes episodic memory for personal events, such as your last birthday, and semantic memory for facts, such as the capital of France. Non-declarative memory is expressed through performance rather than recall. It includes procedural skills like riding a bicycle, which rely heavily on the basal ganglia and cerebellum, and priming, where earlier exposure makes later processing faster.

## Consolidation and reconsolidation

New memories are fragile. Over days to years, memories that first depend on the hippocampus are gradually reorganised so that they rely more on the neocortex, a process called systems consolidation. This is one reason why damage to the hippocampus tends to spare old memories more than recent ones.

Recalling a memory does not simply replay it. When a consolidated memory is retrieved it can become unstable again and must be re-stored, a process called reconsolidation. During that window the memory can be updated or distorted, which helps explain why vivid, confident memories can still be wrong.
