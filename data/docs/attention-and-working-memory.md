# Attention and Working Memory

## How much can we hold in mind?

Working memory is the small mental workspace that holds information while we use it, like a phone number between reading and dialling it. In 1956 George Miller famously suggested a span of seven plus or minus two items. Later work, summarised by Nelson Cowan in 2001, found that when people cannot rehearse or group items, the real capacity is closer to about four chunks.

A chunk is a unit of meaning, so capacity depends on knowledge. The letters F B I C I A are six items to most people but two chunks to someone who reads them as FBI and CIA. Experts in chess or music seem to hold enormous amounts in mind because they chunk familiar patterns. Working memory relies on networks linking the prefrontal and parietal cortex.

## Attention is a filter

The brain receives far more information than it can process, so attention selects what gets through. This selection is powerful enough to make us blind to things in plain view. In a well-known 1999 experiment by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, viewers counting basketball passes often failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. This effect is called inattentional blindness.

## The myth of multitasking

For tasks that need thought, the brain does not truly do two things at once. What feels like multitasking is rapid switching between tasks. Each switch has a cost, because the brain must reconfigure itself for the new task's rules and goals. Experiments show that people are slower and make more errors immediately after switching than when repeating the same task, and the costs grow when the tasks are complex or unfamiliar. Frequent interruptions can therefore make work take longer than doing the tasks one after another.

## The default mode network

When people rest quietly with no task, a particular set of brain regions becomes more active, including parts of the medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate cortex and regions near the hippocampus. Marcus Raichle and colleagues described this pattern in 2001 and named it the default mode network.

The network is active during mind-wandering, daydreaming, remembering the past, imagining the future and thinking about oneself and other people. Its activity tends to fall when we focus on a demanding external task, and it often works in opposition to the networks that control focused attention. A wandering mind is therefore not an idle brain; it is busy with a different kind of work.
