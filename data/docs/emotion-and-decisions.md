# Emotion and Decision Making

## The amygdala and fear

The amygdala is a pair of almond-shaped clusters of neurons deep in the temporal lobes. It helps detect threats and learn which situations predict danger. In fear conditioning, an animal hears a tone just before a mild shock; after a few pairings the tone alone triggers freezing and a racing heart. Joseph LeDoux and others traced this learning to changes in the amygdala, which receives a fast, rough route of sensory information that can trigger a response before the cortex has finished identifying the threat.

## Why emotional events are remembered

Emotional experiences are remembered better than neutral ones. During arousing events the body releases stress hormones such as adrenaline, and noradrenaline acts in the amygdala, which in turn boosts memory storage in the hippocampus and cortex. Work by James McGaugh showed that blocking this signalling weakens the memory advantage for emotional material. This is why people often recall where they were during shocking news, although such flashbulb memories are vivid rather than reliably accurate.

## The prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex, at the front of the frontal lobes, supports executive functions: planning, holding goals in mind, inhibiting impulses and switching strategies when rules change. It also regulates emotional responses, partly through connections that dampen amygdala activity.

In 1848 a railway worker named Phineas Gage survived an accident in which an iron tamping rod passed through his skull and destroyed much of his left frontal lobe. Accounts from the time describe him as having become impulsive and unreliable, although historians note that later reports exaggerated the change and that he partly recovered. His case became an early clue that the frontal lobes shape personality and self-control.

## Emotion is part of reasoning

It is tempting to think that the best decisions are made without emotion. The neurologist Antonio Damasio studied patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex who kept normal intelligence on tests but made disastrous choices in daily life. In a card game called the Iowa gambling task, healthy players learn to avoid risky decks and show stress responses before choosing them, while these patients do not. Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis proposes that bodily feelings tied to past outcomes guide choices, so emotion is a working part of good judgement rather than its enemy.

## The teenage brain

The brain's reward circuits become highly responsive around puberty, while the prefrontal systems that exert control keep maturing well into the twenties, often cited as around age 25. Maturation involves pruning unused synapses and adding myelin to long-range connections. This gap in timing helps explain why adolescents are especially sensitive to rewards and to the presence of peers when taking risks, even though they can reason about risk perfectly well when calm.
