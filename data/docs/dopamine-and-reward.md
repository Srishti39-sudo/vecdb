# Dopamine and Reward

## Where dopamine comes from

Dopamine is made by a small number of neurons, a few hundred thousand in humans, clustered in two midbrain regions: the ventral tegmental area and the substantia nigra. Their axons branch widely, reaching the striatum and the prefrontal cortex, so a small population can broadcast a signal across much of the brain.

## A prediction error, not a pleasure signal

In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys that received drops of juice. When juice arrived unexpectedly, the neurons fired a burst. After the monkeys learned that a light predicted the juice, the burst moved to the light, and the juice itself produced no extra response. When the light came on but the juice was withheld, the neurons briefly fell silent at the moment the juice should have arrived.

This pattern means dopamine reports a reward prediction error: the difference between the reward received and the reward expected. Better than expected produces a burst, as expected produces nothing, and worse than expected produces a dip. The same quantity drives temporal difference learning, an algorithm from machine learning, which is why the finding linked neuroscience and artificial intelligence so closely.

## Wanting versus liking

Popular writing often calls dopamine the pleasure chemical, but research by Kent Berridge suggests that is misleading. Rats whose dopamine was almost completely depleted still showed normal facial expressions of liking when sugar was placed in their mouths, yet they would not work to obtain it. Dopamine appears to drive wanting, the motivation to pursue a reward, more than liking, the pleasure of receiving it. Pleasure itself depends on small hotspots that use opioid and related signalling.

## When the system is hijacked

Addictive drugs such as cocaine and amphetamine raise dopamine levels directly, bypassing the prediction machinery. The brain receives a signal that says this was better than expected every time, so cues linked to the drug acquire powerful pull. With repeated use, wanting can grow even as liking fades.

## When dopamine neurons die

In Parkinson's disease, dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra gradually die. Because these neurons feed circuits in the basal ganglia that help start and scale movement, the main symptoms are slowness, stiffness and tremor. Symptoms usually appear only after a large fraction of these neurons has been lost. The standard treatment, L-DOPA, is a precursor that the brain converts into dopamine, partly restoring the missing signal.
