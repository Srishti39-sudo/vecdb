# The Predictive Brain

## Perception as prediction

A leading framework in neuroscience, called predictive processing or predictive coding, holds that the brain does not passively wait for the senses to report on the world. Instead it constantly generates predictions about what it is about to sense, sends them down the processing hierarchy, and compares them with the incoming signal. Only the mismatch, the prediction error, needs to travel upwards, where it is used to update the brain's model of the world.

An influential computational version was published by Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard in 1999, and Karl Friston later generalised the idea in his free energy principle. On this view, what we experience is the brain's best guess about the causes of its sensory input. The framework is widely used, but how completely it explains brain function is still debated.

## Evidence from illusions

Illusions show predictions at work. In the hollow mask illusion, the inside of a mask looks like a normal face pointing outwards, because the brain's strong expectation that faces are convex overrides the depth cues. When an expected sound in a regular sequence is replaced by a different one, the brain produces a distinct electrical response called the mismatch negativity, even when the person is not paying attention. That response is what a prediction error signal would look like.

## Why you cannot tickle yourself

When you move, your brain predicts the sensory consequences of that movement using a copy of the motor command. The cerebellum is thought to play a central role in these predictions. In 1998 Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith showed that self-produced touch feels less intense than the same touch from outside, because it is predicted and therefore cancelled. When a robot introduced a small delay between a person's movement and the touch, the prediction failed and the touch felt more ticklish.

## Expectations shape experience

Because perception blends prediction with input, expectations can change what we feel. Placebo effects are one example: believing a treatment will work can measurably reduce reported pain, and brain imaging shows changes in pain-related activity alongside the reports. Prediction also explains why a familiar journey feels shorter, and why surprises grab attention: a large prediction error is a signal that the model needs updating.
